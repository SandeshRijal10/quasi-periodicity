CHAPTER 1

A Beautiful Warm-Up: Newton Fractals

Some of the best mathematics starts from a question you could ask in the first week of calculus, yet leads somewhere nobody could have predicted just by looking at the question. This is one such example, and it sets the tone for everything that follows.

The problem: finding roots

Suppose you want to solve an equation $p(z) = 0$, but there's no clean formula for the answer. Newton's method is a way of homing in on a solution by repeated guessing and correcting. Starting from some guess $z_0$, you improve it using the rule:

$$z_{n+1} = z_n - \frac{p(z_n)}{p'(z_n)}$$

The idea is geometric: at your current guess, draw the tangent line to the graph of $p$, and see where that tangent line crosses zero. That crossing point becomes your next, usually much better, guess. Repeat a handful of times, and you typically land extremely close to an actual root.

The polynomial we'll use

We'll apply this to the equation

$$p(z) = z^3 - 1 = 0$$

but working with complex numbers, not just real ones. This matters because a degree-3 polynomial always has exactly 3 roots once complex numbers are allowed — even though, restricted to real numbers, $z^3=1$ has only the one obvious solution $z=1$. The other two roots are complex:

$$z_1 = 1, \qquad z_2 = e^{2\pi i/3}, \qquad z_3 = e^{4\pi i/3}$$

Plotted in the complex plane, these three points sit at the corners of an equilateral triangle, each at distance 1 from the origin.

Differentiating gives $p'(z) = 3z^2$, so Newton's method here becomes the concrete update rule:

$$z_{n+1} = z_n - \frac{z_n^3-1}{3z_n^2}$$

The experiment

Now here's the question that turns this into something worth looking at: take every possible starting guess $z_0$ in some region of the complex plane — think of every pixel on a screen as its own starting point — and run Newton's method from each one. Which of the three roots does it eventually converge to?

Color each starting point according to the answer: one color if it converges to $z_1$, a second color for $z_2$, a third for $z_3$.

If you guessed that this produces three simple, cleanly separated regions — a bit like three overlapping circles — that guess is wrong, and it's wrong in a spectacular way.

Why the boundary is a fractal

The three colored regions — called basins of attraction — interlock in a pattern that never simplifies, no matter how far you zoom in. Two properties make this precise.

Self-similarity Zoom into any point where the three colors meet, and you find the same three-way tangle again, at smaller scale — not a simpler picture, the same picture.
No smooth boundary, anywhere In an ordinary partition of a region — countries on a map, say — the boundary between any two pieces is locally close to a straight line if you zoom in far enough. Here, that never happens: at every point on the boundary between, say, the green and orange regions, the third color (violet) is also arbitrarily close by. There is no small stretch of boundary that is simply "two colors meeting" — all three are always present near any boundary point, at every scale.

This boundary set has a name — the Julia set of the map $z \mapsto z - \dfrac{z^3-1}{3z^2}$ — and it captures exactly the starting points where the long-run outcome is maximally sensitive to the tiniest change in $z_0$: nudge a starting guess near this boundary by an imperceptible amount, and you can flip which root it eventually converges to entirely.

Why we start here

This example is deliberately the first thing in the whole project, for a reason worth stating plainly: everything that follows — quasi-periodic functions, their level sets, the fractal "island" map that the final algorithm produces — shares this same character. A perfectly well-defined, entirely deterministic numerical rule, followed faithfully, can generate a picture of endless intricacy that no amount of staring at the original formula would have suggested. Newton's method on a cubic is the simplest possible setting in which to feel that surprise firsthand, before meeting it again in far more sophisticated form later on.

Compact and Non-Compact Worlds

One idea underlies almost everything in this project: on a bounded, closed space, bad behavior has nowhere to hide. This chapter makes that idea precise, then builds a fully explicit example of what goes wrong the moment you take that boundedness away.

2.1 — Compactness, made precise

A set $K$ is compact if it is both:

$$\textbf{closed} \;(\text{contains all its limit points}) \quad\text{and}\quad \textbf{bounded} \;(\text{fits inside some ball of finite radius})$$
SetBounded?Closed?Compact?
Sphere: $x^2+y^2+z^2=1$✅✅✅
Closed disk: $x^2+y^2 \le 1$✅✅✅
Open disk: $x^2+y^2 < 1$✅❌❌
Hyperbola: $xy=1$❌✅❌
Whole plane $\mathbb{R}^2$❌✅❌

Notice the open disk: it's bounded, but not closed, since it's missing its own boundary circle. A sequence marching toward the edge has a limit point that isn't actually in the set — and that single missing detail is enough to break compactness entirely.

2.2 — Limit points

$$p \text{ is a limit point of } (x_n) \iff \forall\, \varepsilon>0,\;\; \#\{n : |x_n-p|<\varepsilon\} = \infty$$

In words: no matter how tiny a neighborhood you draw around $p$, infinitely many terms of the sequence fall inside it.

Bolzano–Weierstrass $$(x_n) \text{ bounded} \;\Longrightarrow\; (x_n) \text{ has at least one limit point}$$ This is the rigorous version of "on a compact space, there's nowhere to hide."

Four worked cases

How many limit points can one sequence have?

Case 1 — exactly one (convergent)
$$x_n = \frac{1}{n} \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \qquad \text{only limit point: } p=0$$
Case 2 — exactly two (interleaved)
$$x_n = \begin{cases} 1/n & n \text{ even} \\ 1+1/n & n \text{ odd}\end{cases} \qquad \text{limit points: } 0 \text{ and } 1$$
Case 3 — uncountably many (all rationals in [0,1])

Every real number in $[0,1]$ is approachable by rationals — e.g. approaching $\sqrt2-1$:

$$q_1=0.4,\;\; q_2=0.41,\;\; q_3=0.414,\;\; q_4=0.4142,\;\dots \;\longrightarrow\; \sqrt2-1$$

One sequence listing all rationals in $[0,1]$ has every point of $[0,1]$ as a limit point.

Case 4 — none at all (only possible when unbounded)
$$x_n = n \quad(1,2,3,4,\dots) \;\longrightarrow\; \infty \qquad \text{no limit point exists}$$

2.3 — Countable vs. uncountable

$$\text{Countable} = \text{listable as a sequence} = \text{1-to-1 with } \mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}$$
$$\mathbb{Q} \text{ is countable} \qquad\text{(surprising — density} \ne \text{size)}$$
$$[0,1]\subset\mathbb{R} \text{ is uncountable}$$

Sketch of why (diagonalization): suppose every real number in $[0,1]$ could be listed as $r_1,r_2,r_3,\dots$, each an infinite decimal. Build a new number $r^*$ whose $n$-th digit differs from $r_n$'s $n$-th digit. Then $r^*$ differs from every $r_n$ somewhere — so $r^*$ isn't on the list at all, contradicting the assumption that the list was complete.

2.4 — The payoff: a maximum, guaranteed

Theorem

If $f$ is continuous on a compact set $K$, then $f$ attains a maximum on $K$.

Proof, by contradiction

Suppose $f$ has no maximum. Then:

$$\forall n \in \mathbb{N},\; \exists\, x_n \in K \text{ such that } f(x_n) > n$$

This gives a sequence $(x_n)$ inside the bounded set $K$. By Bolzano–Weierstrass, some subsequence converges:

$$x_{n_k} \to p \in K$$

By continuity:

$$f(p) = \lim_{k\to\infty} f(x_{n_k})$$

But $f(x_{n_k}) > n_k \to \infty$, forcing $f(p)=\infty$ — impossible, since continuity requires $f(p)$ to be an actual finite number. Contradiction — so $f$ must have a maximum.

∎

Why this fails on a non-compact set: the same attempted proof breaks exactly at "Bolzano–Weierstrass gives a convergent subsequence" — without boundedness, $(x_n)$ can simply run off to infinity with no accumulation point at all, so no contradiction ever arises. This is precisely why $f(x)=x$ on $\mathbb{R}$ has no maximum: nothing stops $x_n=n$ from escaping to infinity.

2.5 — A fully constructive example

Take the simplest possible vector field: $\dot x = 1,\; \dot y = 0$, whose trajectories are just parallel horizontal lines. Now consider a genuinely different one:

$$\dot x = 2y, \qquad \dot y = 1-y^2$$

On $y=1$: $\dot y=0$, $\dot x=2$ — constant rightward motion. On $y=-1$: $\dot y=0$, $\dot x=-2$ — constant leftward motion. Everywhere else, curves come in from infinity, bend, and go back out, asymptotic to these two special lines on either end.

Two trajectories are separable if small-enough segments crossing each never share a common trajectory. The lines $y=\pm1$ here are inseparable: some in-between trajectory always crosses arbitrarily small segments near both.

Here's the construction that makes this precise and buildable: insert a curve asymptotic to both special lines — approaching one as $x\to-\infty$, the other as $x\to+\infty$ — which makes them inseparable. Then repeat the same trick at smaller and smaller scales, indefinitely:

$$\text{iteration 1} \to \text{iteration 2} \to \text{iteration 3} \to \cdots$$

After infinitely many steps, you get a countable set of pairwise-inseparable lines — enough to be dense, since density only requires countability, not uncountability.

Each iteration makes the field rotate faster in a narrower band, so its derivative diverges as you go to infinity:

$$\|\nabla V(z)\| \to \infty \quad \text{as } |z|\to\infty$$

— finite at every actual point, but unbounded as a limit. On a compact set this would be impossible: the sequence of "turn-around" points would be forced (by §2.2) to converge to some point $p$, where the derivative would then have to be genuinely infinite. On the non-compact plane, that sequence simply escapes to infinity instead, so the contradiction never triggers. See it built step by step below.

This is a schematic, not a literal solution. De Leo's construction was shown only as a picture, and he was explicit that the exact formula doesn't matter — only that one exists. Every connector added here is finite and perfectly well-defined — the field never actually reaches infinite steepness anywhere on the page. But the steepness required keeps climbing without bound the farther out you push. This is the fully concrete version of "hiding pathology at infinity," and it's the single idea — compact spaces leave bad behavior nowhere to go — that everything from here on builds on. Quasi-periodic functions, coming up next, are interesting precisely because they live on the boundary of this idea: not periodic (not living on a genuinely compact circle), yet still inheriting boundedness from a periodic, compact origin.
